Grief can follow the death of someone close, but also other losses such as a relationship ending, losing a job or home, or a loved one becoming seriously ill. The NHS explains that common reactions include shock and numbness, deep sadness and crying, exhaustion, anger, and guilt about things said or left unsaid. Feelings often come in waves, sometimes unexpectedly. Grief can feel chaotic, but it usually becomes less intense over time, and there is no right or wrong way to feel.

Things that may help:

- Talk to someone you trust, such as a friend, family member or counsellor.
- Consider peer support with others who have been through something similar.
- Exercise regularly, which can lift your mood and help you sleep.
- Eat regular, healthy meals to keep your energy steady.

Things to try to avoid:

- Trying to do everything at once — set small, achievable goals.
- Dwelling on things you can't change.
- Thinking you're alone; grief is common and support is available.
- Using alcohol, smoking, gambling or drugs to cope, as these can make you feel worse.

If you're struggling to cope, your low mood lasts more than two weeks, or intense grief doesn't ease over months and makes daily life hard, speak to a GP or a talking therapies service — and get urgent help straight away if you're in crisis or having thoughts of suicide.
