---
title: Meditation and Mindfulness — What Research Suggests
source: NCCIH — Meditation and Mindfulness: Effectiveness and Safety
url: https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-effectiveness-and-safety
---
The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) notes that much of the research on meditation and mindfulness is preliminary or not very rigorous, so results should be read with some caution.

What studies suggest so far:

- **Stress, anxiety and low mood:** Mindfulness approaches have generally done better than no treatment, and in some analyses about as well as established talking therapies. Some benefits for anxiety appear short-term.
- **Sleep:** Mindfulness meditation improved sleep quality more than education-based approaches, and about as much as exercise.
- **Pain:** Findings are mixed. There is some evidence for short-term help with low-back pain and for better pain tolerance, but not consistent reductions in pain.
- **Eating habits:** Mindfulness seems more useful for managing eating behaviors than for weight loss.

Safety: these practices are usually considered low-risk, but few studies have looked carefully for harms. One large review estimated that around 8% of participants had a negative experience, most often increased anxiety or low mood, a rate similar to other psychological approaches.

Practical tips from NCCIH: don't use meditation to replace conventional care or delay seeing a provider, ask about an instructor's training and experience, and tell your health care providers about any complementary practices you use.

If meditation brings up distressing feelings, or symptoms persist, talk with a qualified health care professional.
