Relaxation techniques aim to bring on the body's "relaxation response" — slower breathing, a lower heart rate and lower blood pressure — which is the opposite of the stress response. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) describes several common types:

- **Progressive muscle relaxation:** tense one muscle group, then release the tension, working through different parts of the body in turn.
- **Breathing exercises:** usually slow, deep breathing from the belly (diaphragmatic breathing).
- **Guided imagery:** picturing calm scenes or events and letting your body take on that calm feeling.
- **Autogenic training:** mental exercises using self-suggestion to focus on feelings of relaxation, such as warmth or heaviness in the body.
- **Self-hypnosis:** learning to bring on relaxation with a chosen phrase or cue.

Research is still limited and many studies are small, but there is some evidence these techniques may help with stress and anxiety; for example, early research suggests slow belly breathing may reduce stress.

Relaxation techniques are generally considered safe for healthy people. Some people notice more anxiety, intrusive thoughts or a fear of losing control while practising, and rarely they can worsen symptoms in people with a history of trauma or certain health conditions — if a technique feels uncomfortable, stop or try a different one.

If anxiety or low mood persists, or your symptoms are severe or long-lasting, seek help from a qualified health care professional.
